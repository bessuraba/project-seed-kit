# How to run early customer-discovery interviews — The Mom Test

**Reference material — rarely changes.** Fills a real gap: "go run primary interviews" is common
advice, but rarely comes with a method for actually doing that well. This is it.

**Source**: *The Mom Test* by Rob Fitzpatrick — confirmed via [momtestbook.com](https://www.momtestbook.com/)
as the standard, still-in-print practitioner reference; used in Harvard/UCL curricula and startup
accelerators (Seedcamp, Microsoft Ventures) per the book's own page. Chosen because it's the
most-cited practical (not academic) framework specifically for early, pre-product customer
conversations — pairs directly with Lean Startup/Customer Development and JTBD in
`product-discovery.md`, which name the *need* for these interviews but not *how* to run them well.

## The core problem it solves

People are reflexively polite and will say what they think you want to hear about your idea —
compliments, "I'd definitely use that," vague enthusiasm — none of which predicts whether they'd
actually pay or change behavior. "Mom test": a question is bad if your own mother, trying to be
supportive, could still lie to you and have it sound like validation.

## Three core rules

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Don't pitch or describe the product. Ask about their
   current behavior, workflow, and problems instead — what they already do, not what they'd think
   of something new.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or opinions about the future.** "Would you use
   X?" and "Do you think this is a good idea?" produce fiction. "Tell me about the last time you
   ran into [problem]" produces fact. Past behavior predicts; hypothetical opinions don't.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** Follow-up questions ("why did that happen," "what did you do next,"
   "what else have you tried") over pitching. If you're doing most of the talking, you're not
   learning anything.

## Good data vs. bad data (how to tell if an interview actually worked)

**Bad data (compliments/fluff/fiction):** "That's a great idea." "I would definitely buy that."
"I'd probably use it once a week." None of these cost the person anything to say.

**Good data (commitments and hard facts):** A concrete past example with specifics (what happened,
what they did, what it cost them). A real commitment that costs something now — money, an intro to
a real customer, a scheduled next meeting, a beta-access signup with a real email. If the
conversation ends with only warm words and no ask made or commitment given, treat it as no signal,
not weak-positive signal.

## Applying it

Run this against whatever hypothesis set secondary research already produced — pain points are
things to verify happened to the person, in their own past words, not things to describe and ask
"does this resonate?" about. Concretely: don't ask "would X data be useful to you?" — ask "tell me
about the last time you faced [the underlying problem], and how you handled it" and let the
hypothesis surface (or not) on its own.
