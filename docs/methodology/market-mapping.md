# Market mapping — systematically finding all players in a category

**Reference material — rarely changes.** How to discover a category's players systematically,
instead of ad hoc googling until it feels complete.

**Source**: "Competitive Landscape Mapping Framework," documented by consulting-tools aggregator
[Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/competitive-landscape-mapping-framework/)
(used by ex-McKinsey/Bain/BCG consultants) and
[Bridgespan](https://www.bridgespan.org/insights/market-mapping-and-landscape-analysis) (nonprofit
strategy consultancy) — practitioner-standard, not academic-only.

## The process

1. **Define objectives** — what question must the map answer? ("Which platforms should we scrape"
   is a valid objective.)
2. **Define market boundary** — draw the category line explicitly (e.g. "marketplaces selling
   templates/plugins/digital design assets to individual buyers," excluding SaaS tools, physical
   goods, general e-commerce).
3. **Collect data** — comparison/roundup articles ("best X marketplaces"), directories, "X vs Y vs
   Z" content, adjacent-platform cross-links (a platform's own comparison pages naming competitors).
4. **Inventory systematically** — not just direct competitors: substitutes, potential entrants,
   complements, gatekeepers. Categorize by dimensions that matter (price tier, channel, seller
   segment, business model — subscription vs. per-item, curated vs. open).
5. **Plot and synthesize** — lay entries out by the dimensions from step 4.
6. **Identify white space** — attribute combinations no player occupies. This is the actual
   strategic payoff, not just a longer list.

## Competition intensity — Porter's Five Forces

Once players are inventoried, [Porter's Five
Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis) (Michael Porter, HBR 1979)
gives a structured qualitative read on how competitive a category actually is: rivalry among
existing players, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, buyer bargaining power (how easy
is it for a seller to switch marketplaces), supplier bargaining power (here: the pool of creators
themselves). Useful as a checklist before calling a category "saturated" or "open" — each force is a
separate question, not one gut read.

## Competitor profile table format

For step 5 ("plot and synthesize") applied to named competitors specifically, not just market
categories: standard practice across product-management templates (Aha!, Productboard, airfocus,
aakashg's free PM template) is a **profile table** — one row per competitor, same columns for every
row: pricing, top features/output shape, strengths, weaknesses/gap vs. us, target market, scale
(users/revenue), source. Keep this to **3-5 direct/indirect competitors** — a longer list dilutes
the comparison; put everything else in a lighter **scan table** (name, category, shape, scale) for
pattern-spotting instead of full profiles. For feature-level comparison specifically, a **matrix**
(features as rows, competitors as columns, yes/no/partial cells) beats prose — visual scanability
over paragraphs. Source:
[competitor analysis best practices, aggregated](https://www.aakashg.com/competitive-analysis-template-free/)
(SaaS-specific column/matrix guidance) and
[Productboard's competitor analysis guide](https://www.productboard.com/glossary/competitor-analysis/).
