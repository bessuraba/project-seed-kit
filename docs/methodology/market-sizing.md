# Market sizing, competition, and growth — three separate measures

**Reference material — rarely changes.** Three distinct, named methods for the three questions:
how big, how competitive, and is it growing. Don't conflate them — each answers a different
question and uses different inputs.

## Size — bottom-up TAM (Bill Aulet, *Disciplined Entrepreneurship*, MIT, 2013)

The bootstrapped/indie-suited alternative to VC-style top-down TAM (start from a big analyst number,
slice it down). Instead: count actual identifiable end users in a narrow segment (an "end-user
density" unit — e.g. number of sellers on a specific platform) and multiply by realistic
revenue-per-user. Aulet's own guidance: a good beachhead TAM is "attractive but not so large it's
unfocused," built from primary/countable data, not extrapolation from someone else's headline
number. Source: [Disciplined Entrepreneurship Workbook — Step 4](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/disciplined-entrepreneurship-workbook/9781119365792/c04.xhtml).

**Why this over generic TAM/SAM/SOM**: a platform's own "X million members" claim (common in
marketplace marketing copy) is not a TAM — it's an unverified vanity number. Aulet's method forces a
countable, defensible number instead (e.g. "N active sellers on this specific marketplace" ×
"realistic price a seller would pay for saturation/pricing data").

## Competition — Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)

The standard quantitative concentration measure, used by antitrust regulators (US DOJ/FTC): sum of
squared market shares of every firm in the market. Ranges 0 (many equal-size players, highly
competitive/fragmented) to 10,000 (monopoly). DOJ/FTC bands: <1,500 unconcentrated, 1,500-2,500
moderately concentrated, >2,500 highly concentrated. Source: [DOJ Antitrust Division —
HHI](https://www.justice.gov/atr/herfindahl-hirschman-index).

**Why this over eyeballing "feels saturated"**: gives an actual number instead of a vibe. Requires
estimating relative shares (e.g. top-N sellers' share of listings or sales on a marketplace) —
computable from real scraped/observed data once a category is in scope, not something that needs
external market-research spend.

## Growth — BCG growth axis / Product Life Cycle stage

**BCG Growth-Share Matrix's growth axis** (Bruce Henderson, BCG, 1970) — even used standalone
(without the market-share axis), "market growth rate" is a named, citable way to classify a category
as high-growth, flat, or declining. **Product Life Cycle** (Theodore Levitt, HBR 1965:
introduction/growth/maturity/decline) is the complementary qualitative read — judge a category's
stage from signals like new-entrant rate, price competition intensity, and volume trend, not just a
single growth-rate number. Sources: [Umbrex — BCG Growth-Share
Matrix](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/bcg-growth-share-matrix/),
Levitt's original: "Exploiting the Product Life Cycle," *Harvard Business Review*, 1965.
